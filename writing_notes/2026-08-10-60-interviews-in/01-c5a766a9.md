If you're in the tech industry and hear the word "interviews", I'm willing to bet you either thinking "please don't remind me" or "I'm so glad I'm not doing that right now".

Some people pass them with eyes closed. I even have a friend who legitimately enjoys doing them, like solving a puzzle. For the rest of us, it's a brutal, nerve racking, anxiety reaking experience.

If you're going through it, or thinking about it, this post is going to capture my recent experience and reflections on today's interview landscape. For obvious reasons, I can't share question details or company names, but I aim to provide both breadth and depth of how things operate today.

I'm personally very grateful for those who helped me get through it, and couldn't be more excied for the role I landed. I legitimatel can't think of more exciting work to do today, and I hope you'll find your role as well. If you want to reach out after reading through this post, I left details towards the end.

## A bit of Background about me

After winding down my last company, I did a little bit of consulting in looking for ways to adopt AI in the geotechnica and construction industries. I realized there's a huge opportuntiy, but I know what it takes to get a company off the ground, and it'snot what I was looking for right now.

I was looking for a place where I can work on the most important problems in today's world, alongside an awesome team and compound, and create real impact. I want to compound on the technical, economical, and cultular foundation someone else has established. Creating real economic value in driving the adoption of AI to real users is the clearest path I could see to doing so, and I didn't need to do it alone.

## Leveling

There is some variability between levels across large companies, and startup leveling is just a throw of the dice.

If you're intervieing at a large company, you likely know the level you are going for: Entry, Mid, Senior, Staff, Sr. Staff, Principal.

This part gets a little tricky and is a double edged sword.

If you've operated as a Staff engineer for a significant amount of time, it's kind of awkward to interview you for a senior role. Your compensation expectations won't be matched, and there are plenty of other candidates who are more fitting for that role.

At the same time, I'm confident saying that most individuals at any company will not pass their company's interview loop for a specific role that day.

We find ourselves in a conundrum where you have to perform at your absolute best at a specific time, on a specific day, talking to a specific individual, solving a specific problem.

It is much easier to get promoted into a particular role than to interview and get hired for it.

## Failure Modes

**Energy Level**: The interviewer has a lot of things top of mind and is likely jumping between meetings, messages or some work before they get on a call wit you. For them, it's one of N things they're doing that day. For you, it is likely your entire day. It's what you think about before the interview, and what you think about for the rest of the day after.

**Improvising**: Some interviewers follow a very strict script, others follow no script. Sometimes going off script can result in an amazing interview, other times it could lead to nowhere. Ideally the latter should never happen, but there is a reason why ideals are not reality.

**Blanking**: I had a coding interview where I blanked and couldn't solve something obvious. It was simulatenously dissapointing and emberrasing. After the call ended, I went on a 10 minute walk, returned, and sped through it.

**Shy Coder**: I don't like people watching me code. When you pair with a teammate, you have rapport and baseline trust with someone else. During the interview, it is literally the other person's job to judge you. Some people have shy bladders, some people are shy coders. This sucks, and each company needs to decide if it's something they want to incorporate in their loop.

## There's no bad interviewer, just a bad interviewee

If they're less experienced than you - show them how much experience you have, and how much you'll help them grow.

If they're more experienced than you - show them what you know that they don't, and how much you'll learn.

If they're asking "the wrong question" or one you don't have experience with - guide them to a question wher eyou can showcase your skills and abilitiles.

If they're not clear - clarify.

Always, no matter what, go above and beyond. Teach your interviewer something new, and leave a lasting impression.

There's the calssic, "it's a two way interview", and it is, but the power dynamic is a shfiting one depening on the interveews cirumstance.

## Raw material

- Everyone says to do practice interview, and they're right. But, it's a waste of everyone's time. Any time I went into interviewing with a company, I studied the culture, the projects, the team. I embedded myself into their culture without being there. Otherwise, how else can you be part of it?
- That time a co-founder reached out to me on LinkedIn and I still couldn't get a resume
- Emailing a CTO and only getting on a call with someone a month later
- Getting my interviews rescheduled a dozen times
- Don't be susceptible to the pressure
- These are not interviews, they are tests. As much as someone wants to say "I want to see how you think", that only comes after you pass the test.
- I remember when people used to say "tell me if you've seen this question before", because the goal was to see how you think, not how you answer the question.
- Interviews turned from university admissions into SATs.
- The bar is not higher, but the margin for error is much lower. No one even asks "have you seen this question before".
- Preparing for interviews is less about showing what you can do, and more about showing you're willing to put in the time and effort.
- An interview is like an Olympic performance. You bring everything you have into a very short time period.
- A lot of things need to align to pass an interview: moods, minds, emotions, timing.

## Mental atrophy and ego

- My ego got big. I gave people optionality. At one point in life I was better at the process, but at a much more junior level. Then I became a "thought leader", developed an ego, and thought I wouldn't have to do this again.
- Mental atrophy is real and people don't realize it.
- So many people get into senior leadership but wouldn't pass a senior interview. Yes — you make decisions, you take on responsibility, you have agency, you move the needle — but we have to decide if interviews are "interviews" or if they're "tests".
- Everyone should do interviews at least once every 1.5 years. If you're a "Greg Brockman"-like figure it might be harder, but I'd challenge him to do it too.
- People like to talk about the physical challenges, the new skills, the challenges they struggle with but want to do. How about the challenge you *don't* want to do that everyone else has to come across?
- Don't talk about how interviews are broken — do them.
- See Steve Yegge's post about Google (in [Resources](#resources)): those were our own interview packets from when we had all interviewed at Google. The recruiters had tricked us into reviewing our own packets, and we had voted not to hire most of our own group.

## What actually matters

- Vibe alignment

## What could replace it

- Work trials, but they don't scale. To me it's like capitalism: a flawed system, but it beats the alternatives we have.
- We do internships for students — why not work trials for seniors? No equity, just cash. If it works, bring them in on the date of their first day. Firing is easy — you don't have to pay severance.

## Asking better questions

- Don't say "tell me about a time you gave someone feedback" or "how would others describe you". You'll get a prepared answer if you ask a prepared question.
- Ask instead:
  - "So which job did you do X in?"
  - "What was the composition of that team?"
  - "Did you ever have to give someone feedback? What was it?"

# Ways I've failed

- I was never good at interviews. I was always just good enough to pass the bar and show enough signal.
